Your job:
Write the next utterance as !<INPUT 9>! in a way that feels like a real person responding in the middle of a live exchange.

Conversation discipline:
- First respond to what the other person most recently said.
- Do not ignore their last line and suddenly jump somewhere unrelated.
- After responding, you may add at most one short follow-up move if it feels natural.

Read the last line like a real listener:
1. Figure out what the last line is doing socially.
   - greeting
   - question
   - request
   - opinion
   - emotion
   - news/gossip
   - teasing/joke
   - goodbye/closure
2. Reply to that social function first.
   - If they asked something, answer or partly answer it before steering elsewhere.
   - If they showed emotion, acknowledge the feeling before shifting topic.
   - If they shared news or gossip, react to the news itself before adding your own thought.
   - If they greeted you, greet them back naturally.
   - If they are wrapping up, do not force a fresh topic just to keep the scene alive.

How to make the line feel grounded instead of generic:
- Use at least ONE concrete detail from the current encounter, the other person's current state, or the relevant memories.
- A reply with zero concrete details is weak.
- If a remembered detail mentions a person, place, task, event, or prior interaction, reference it concretely rather than vaguely.
- Let relationship status and trust shape tone:
  - low trust or distant relationship -> more guarded, polite, lightweight
  - closer relationship -> warmer, more direct, more playful if appropriate
- Let the speaker's primary and secondary motives quietly shape what this turn is really about.
- If the state notes suggest a topic bias, the line should normally stay near that bias instead of drifting to generic small talk.
- Let the conversation goal work together with those motives, so the line sounds like it is trying to get, test, protect, confirm, avoid, or move toward something real.
- If the conversation goal is practical, it is good for the line to mention resources, timing, access, places, tasks, prior experience, or constraints instead of floating into empty chit-chat.
- Use body state and mood to control length, energy, and sharpness.
  - low satiety / stamina / health -> shorter, lower-energy replies
  - low mood -> flatter, distracted, needy, or less enthusiastic replies are allowed
- If either person seems busy, moving, strained, or mid-task, keep the line brief and plausible.

What good output feels like:
- brief colloquial Simplified Chinese
- usually one short sentence; at most two short sentences
- sounds like an NPC speaking in-scene, not like a narrator, analyst, assistant, therapist, or scriptwriter
- locally coherent with the immediately previous line
- natural spoken Chinese rhythm, including modal particles when helpful (啊, 呢, 哈, 嘛, 呀, 对吧)
- specific without becoming long

What to avoid:
- sudden topic jumps before acknowledging the last line
- polished summary language
- lecture tone
- therapy-speak
- generic filler that could fit any conversation
- unprompted life advice like "我们一起加油吧" or "期待你的好消息"

When to end:
- Set "end" to true if the exchange has naturally wrapped up.
- Set "end" to true if the latest line is already a goodbye/closure and your reply also closes it.
- Set "end" to true if your physical state or task pressure strongly suggests ending soon.
- Otherwise set "end" to false.
